The formula

  1. Free cash flow in year tFCF(t) = FCF(0) × (1 + g)^t
  2. Present value of the growth yearsPV(growth) = Σ FCF(t) ÷ (1 + r)^t, t = 1 … N
  3. Terminal value at the end of year NTV = FCF(N) × (1 + gT) ÷ (r − gT)
  4. Present value of the terminal valuePV(TV) = TV ÷ (1 + r)^N
  5. Equity valueE = PV(growth) + PV(TV) + cash − debt
  6. Intrinsic value per shareV = E ÷ shares
  7. Margin of safetyMoS = (V − price) ÷ V

FCF(0) is the last 12 months of free cash flow, g the growth rate, N the number of growth years, gT the terminal growth rate, and r the discount rate. The model needs r to be higher than gT.

How to read the result

It is an estimate, not a forecast. The answer is only as good as the assumptions. Change the growth rate or the discount rate by one point and watch how far the value moves.

Watch the terminal value. When most of the value comes from the years after the growth period, the estimate rests on what happens far in the future, which is the part nobody knows well.

Margin of safety. Value investors compare the estimate with the price and look for a gap wide enough to cover their own mistakes. A small or negative gap means the assumptions leave no room for error.

Where it works poorly. A DCF suits companies with steady, positive free cash flow. It says little about banks, insurers, or companies that are not yet producing cash.
